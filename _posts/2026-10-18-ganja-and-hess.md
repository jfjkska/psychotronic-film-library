Ganja & Hess (1973) is written and directed by Bill Gunn, who also appears in
the film. It is hailed in academic circles as a bona fide cult classic and
a culturally important Black-produced film. Marlene Clark and Duane Jones
star as the aforementioned Ganja and Hess. Jones was famous for his man of
action lead role in Romero's groundbreaking Night of the Living Dead.

The plot concerns Dr Hess Green, who is stabbed with a Myrthian ceremonial
dagger by his unstable assistant George Meda (Gunn). Meda shoots himself
dead after stabbing Hess, but the good doctor finds himself not only alive
but able to enjoy eternal life. There is a downside: he needs human blood
to live. Meda's wife enters the film and soon learns that George is dead
and Hess is, in all respects, a vampire. That doesn't stop her marrying
Hess and becoming the same as him.

Gunn doesn't so much direct as seem to point the camera and shoot whatever
is happening in front of it. It is so laboured and slow that the film takes
on a hazy, dreamlike texture. It's as if Jean Rollin had upped sticks and
moved to the States to direct a blaxploitation film.

The scenes in the church with the Baptist congregation do add a brief
upbeat to a very downbeat 113 minutes. The use of blues, gospel and soul is
a welcome musical brush to colour the monotony. It may be a classic but I
will not be returning, even if I live for eternity.
