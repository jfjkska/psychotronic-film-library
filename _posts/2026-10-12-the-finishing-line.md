The Finishing Line (1977) was commissioned by British Transport Films and
directed by John Krish. Not strictly a public information film, it has all
the aspects you would expect from this type of film. It opens with an
authoritative voice saying, "The railway is not the place for games, the
railway is not the games field."

A young boy perched on a bridge over the train track imagines what a
sports day would look like on the railway track. What follows is a
dramatisation of his thoughts: brass bands, a scoreboard, marquees, a
gathered crowd, a PA system and children divided into four coloured teams.

The games begin and the horror unfolds in graphic detail. Children are
left on the track to be run over by an approaching train. Rocks are hurled
through train windows, and passengers and a driver are bleeding from their
heads after being struck by the rocks. Teams of children have to cross the
line as a speeding train approaches. "Just run when the train comes, do as
I say," a team leader barks to his peers. More casualties are stretchered
away after each game; the number of dead is adding up.

Finally comes the Great Tunnel Walk: "3 miles of dark, smelly tunnel and
once you are in, there is no turning back." Three children emerge at the
other end, bloody and crippled. They are the lucky winners. Adults bring
out the rest, all dead, over shoulders and in arms. The dead children are
laid out on the train track. There are scores of them, their white sports
kit stained with blood. The brass band plays a solemn lament, and the film
closes with the authoritative voice of the school headteacher once again
reminding the viewer that railways are not for games.

The Finishing Line was shown on British television on several occasions.
It proved so controversial it was banned for twenty years. The ban was
eventually lifted in 1997. It remains a difficult watch but also a stark
reminder of the dangers of playing near and on a railway line.
